# The number system

## Why this matters

On a Sunday morning the worship leader turns round and says "1-4-5, but take it to the 6 on the last line". Nobody says "C, F, G, Am", because the song might be in C today and in Eb next week for a different lead singer. Players who think in numbers follow along in any key; players who only learnt chord shapes from one video get lost the moment the key changes. Producers get the same benefit: a progression you know as numbers can be dropped into any key a vocalist or sample needs.

## Numbers are scale degrees

You already know how to build a major scale. Number its notes from 1 to 7 and you have the number system. In C major:

```
| C | D | E | F | G | A | B | C |
  1   2   3   4   5   6   7   1
```

In G major the numbers stay in the same places, and only the letters change:

```
| G | A | B | C | D | E | F#| G |
  1   2   3   4   5   6   7   1
```

So "the 5" means the fifth note of whatever key you are in: G in C major, D in G major, E in A major. When someone says "play the 5 chord", they mean the chord built on that note.

> **Try it:** Play the C major scale slowly and say the numbers out loud as you go. Then do the same in G major, remembering F#. The numbers you say are the same both times.

## Which chords are major and which are minor

Build a triad on every note of the major scale, using only notes from that scale (stack every other note: 1-3-5, 2-4-6, and so on). The result is always the same pattern of qualities:

```
Number:    1      2      3      4      5      6      7
Quality:   major  minor  minor  major  major  minor  diminished

In C:      C      Dm     Em     F      G      Am     Bdim
In G:      G      Am     Bm     C      D      Em     F#dim
In D:      D      Em     F#m    G      A      Bm     C#dim
```

Learn that middle line by heart: **1, 4 and 5 are major. 2, 3 and 6 are minor. 7 is diminished.** It holds in every major key, because every major scale has the same pattern of steps (W W H W W W H), so the triads on each degree always come out the same shape.

You can check one. The 2 chord in C is D-F-A. From D up to F is 3 half steps (D, D#, E, F), which is a minor third. From F up to A is 4 half steps. Minor third then major third: a minor triad.

```
D  ->  F   = 3 half steps (minor third)
F  ->  A   = 4 half steps (major third)
Result: D minor (Dm)
```

The 7 chord, B-D-F, is two minor thirds stacked (3 + 3). That shape is called diminished, written `Bdim` or `B°`. It is the one you will use least in pop and church music, but it shows up in passing.

> **Note:** On many charts a minor chord is written with a dash in numbers: "6-" means "6 minor". Since 6 is already minor in a major key, people often just write "6" and expect you to know.

## Roman numerals: the same idea

Classical theory books and many YouTube teachers use Roman numerals instead of digits. Capital letters mean major, small letters mean minor, and a small circle means diminished:

```
Digits:   1    2    3    4    5    6    7
Roman:    I    ii   iii  IV   V    vi   vii°
```

So "I-V-vi-IV" and "1-5-6-4" are the same progression. Roman numerals carry the quality in the case of the letters, which is handy on paper. Digits are faster to say and to call out on stage. Learn to read both; you will meet both.

## Why church musicians use numbers

Three practical reasons:

1. **Keys change all the time.** A choir may sing a song in Ab on Sunday and in F at a wedding where the soloist sings lower. The numbers do not change, so the band does not have to relearn the song.
2. **Calling changes is fast.** A music director can hold up four fingers to mean "go to the 4", and every player knows what to do, whatever key they are in.
3. **It trains your ear.** After a while you stop hearing "C to F" and start hearing "1 to 4". That sound is the same in every key, so a song you work out once by ear is a song you can play anywhere.

The Nashville number system used by session players works on the same principle, with a few extra symbols for rhythm. You don't need those yet.

## The primary chords: 1, 4 and 5

The three major chords in a key (1, 4 and 5) are called the **primary chords**. Together they contain every note of the major scale:

```
1 chord (C):  C  E  G
4 chord (F):  F  A  C
5 chord (G):  G  B  D

Together:     C  D  E  F  G  A  B   -> the whole C major scale
```

That is why so many hymns, children's songs, blues tunes and simple praise songs can be played with only three chords. If a melody uses only notes of the scale, one of those three chords will fit under almost any note of it.

> **Try it:** In the key of D, play D, G and A (1, 4, 5) in this order: 1-4-1-5-1. Then do the same in F: F, Bb, C. It is the same sound, moved.

## The four-chord progression

Add the 6 chord to the primary chords and you get four chords that power a huge amount of pop, worship and Afro-pop music. The most common order is **1-5-6-4**:

```
In C:   C  -  G  -  Am  -  F
In G:   G  -  D  -  Em  -  C
In D:   D  -  A  -  Bm  -  G
```

Other common orders use the same four chords: 6-4-1-5 (Am F C G in C) feels darker because it starts on the minor chord, and 1-6-4-5 (C Am F G) has an older, doo-wop feel. Because these orders are so common, the fastest way to start working out a song by ear is to ask "is this the 1, 4, 5 or 6?".

## Working out a progression in a new key

Here is the method, step by step, using 1-4-5 in D as the example:

```
Step 1. Write the major scale of the key:   D  E  F#  G  A  B  C#
Step 2. Number it:                            1  2  3   4  5  6  7
Step 3. Pick the numbers you need:          1 = D, 4 = G, 5 = A
Step 4. Add the quality from the pattern:   1, 4, 5 are major -> D  G  A
```

And 2-5-1 in C:

```
Scale:     C  D  E  F  G  A  B
2 = D, which is minor  -> Dm
5 = G, which is major  -> G
1 = C, which is major  -> C
Result:   Dm  G  C
```

At first you will write the scale out every time. After a few weeks you will know the common keys (C, G, D, F, Bb, Eb, Ab) well enough to skip that step.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the minor chords.** Writing "1-5-6-4 in G" as G D E C sounds wrong because the 6 chord is minor: it is Em, not E.
- **Misspelling the scale.** In F major the 4 is Bb, not A#. Use one letter per degree: F G A Bb C D E.
- **Counting from 0.** The key note is 1, not 0. The next note up is 2.
- **Treating the 7 chord like the others.** It is diminished, not minor. In C it is B-D-F, not B-D-F#.
- **Mixing up the numbers and the chord names when someone calls out a change.** "Go to the 4" means the chord on the fourth note, not the fourth chord you played.

## Recap

- Number the notes of the major scale 1 to 7. The numbers mean the same thing in every key.
- In a major key: 1, 4, 5 are major; 2, 3, 6 are minor; 7 is diminished.
- Roman numerals (I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii°) are the same idea, with case showing the quality.
- The primary chords 1, 4 and 5 hold every note of the scale.
- 1-5-6-4 and its reorderings are the most common progressions you will meet.
- To play a numbered progression in a new key: write the scale, number it, pick the notes, add the quality.
