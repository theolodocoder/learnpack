# The major scale formula

## Why this matters

Almost everything a church keyboardist or producer plays is built on a major scale: the melody notes, the chords, and the "1 4 5" numbers a band uses to call a progression. If you know one formula, you can build the major scale in any key a singer asks for, instead of only knowing the two or three keys you learned by ear. Spotify's five-year Nigeria report found gospel and praise streams in the country rose 5,499% from 2021 to 2025; a lot of that music is played by keyboardists who can move a song into any key on the spot. This lesson gives you the tool to do the same.

## What a scale is

A **scale** is a set of notes, in order, that a piece of music mostly uses. Songs do not use all twelve notes equally. They pick seven of them and lean on those. The scale is that set of seven.

The **major scale** is the one most Western pop, gospel, hymn and Afrobeats music is built on. Its sound is often described as bright or settled. Play the white keys from C to the next C and you have heard it:

```
C  D  E  F  G  A  B  C
1  2  3  4  5  6  7  8(1)
```

The numbers under the notes are the **scale degrees**. The first note (here C) is the **root** or **tonic**: the note that sounds like home. Musicians in gospel bands call chords and notes by these numbers, so "go to the 4" in C means F.

## The formula

Why does C to C on the white keys sound "major"? Because of the gaps between the notes. Count them in half steps:

```
C   D   E   F   G   A   B   C
  W   W   H   W   W   W   H

W = whole step (2 half steps)
H = half step  (1 half step)
```

Remember from the last section that E–F and B–C are half steps. That is exactly where the H steps fall. Every other gap is a whole step.

So the major scale formula is:

```
Major scale = W W H W W W H
            = 2 2 1 2 2 2 1   (in half steps, total 12)
```

Start on **any** note, follow that pattern, and you get the major scale of that note. That is the whole trick.

> **Try it:** Play C D E F G A B C slowly, saying "whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half" as you move. Feel where your fingers move to the next key (half) and where they skip one (whole).

## Building G major

Start on G and apply W W H W W W H:

```
G  -W->  A  -W->  B  -H->  C  -W->  D  -W->  E  -W->  F#  -H->  G
```

E up a whole step is F#, not F, because E–F is only a half step. So G major has one black key:

```
G major: G  A  B  C  D  E  F#
```

## Building D major

```
D  -W->  E  -W->  F#  -H->  G  -W->  A  -W->  B  -W->  C#  -H->  D

D major: D  E  F#  G  A  B  C#
```

Two black keys: F# and C#.

## Building F major

```
F  -W->  G  -W->  A  -H->  Bb  -W->  C  -W->  D  -W->  E  -H->  F

F major: F  G  A  Bb  C  D  E
```

The fourth note is a half step above A. That key is both A# and Bb. So which do you write?

## One letter per degree

Here is the rule that settles it: **a major scale uses each letter A to G exactly once.** Seven notes, seven letters.

In F major the notes so far are F, G, A, and the next one needs the letter after A, which is **B**. So the note is **Bb**, not A#. If you wrote A#, the scale would have two As (A and A#) and no B at all.

```
Wrong:  F  G  A  A#  C  D  E     (two As, no B)
Right:  F  G  A  Bb  C  D  E     (one of each letter)
```

The same rule is why D major has F# (not Gb) and C# (not Db): the letters must run D E F G A B C.

```
D major letters:  D  E  F  G  A  B  C
add the formula:  D  E  F# G  A  B  C#
```

A simple method for any key:

```
1. Write the seven letters in order, starting on the root.
2. Apply W W H W W W H.
3. Add a sharp or flat to each letter so the gaps match.
```

> **Note:** This is also why a scale never mixes sharps and flats. A major key uses sharps only, or flats only (C major uses neither). If you find yourself writing both, check your letters.

## More keys

Here are the major scales you will use most, written correctly:

```
C   major:  C   D   E   F   G   A   B
G   major:  G   A   B   C   D   E   F#
D   major:  D   E   F#  G   A   B   C#
A   major:  A   B   C#  D   E   F#  G#
E   major:  E   F#  G#  A   B   C#  D#
F   major:  F   G   A   Bb  C   D   E
Bb  major:  Bb  C   D   Eb  F   G   A
Eb  major:  Eb  F   G   Ab  Bb  C   D
Ab  major:  Ab  Bb  C   Db  Eb  F   G
```

Many worship and gospel songs sit in the flat keys at the bottom of that list (Bb, Eb, Ab), so it is worth getting your hands used to them early.

> **Try it:** Pick one key from the list that you do not usually play. Play its scale up and down three times, saying the note names out loud. Then play the root, the 4 and the 5 of that key one after another. You will hear the outline of countless songs.

## Scale degrees in practice

The numbers matter as much as the names. In any major key:

```
1 = root (home)
4 = the "four" chord's root
5 = the "five" chord's root, which pulls back to 1
7 = the leading note, a half step below home
```

In G, the 5 is D and the 7 is F#. In Bb, the 5 is F and the 7 is A. Learn the numbers now, and the chords and progressions sections will feel familiar.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the wrong letter name.** F major has Bb, not A#. Check that every letter A to G appears exactly once.
- **Forgetting E–F and B–C are half steps.** A whole step up from E is F#, and from B is C#.
- **Mixing sharps and flats in one scale.** A major scale uses one or the other.
- **Not checking the way back home.** The final H step takes you from the 7th back to the root an octave up. If the 7th is not a half step below the root, a note before it is wrong.
- **Only learning by shape.** Shapes help, but say the note names too. Naming the notes is what lets you transpose and read charts.

## Recap

- The major scale formula is W W H W W W H (2 2 1 2 2 2 1 half steps).
- Start on any note and follow the formula to get that note's major scale.
- Each letter A to G appears once, which decides whether a black key is written as a sharp or a flat.
- G major has F#. D major has F# and C#. F major has Bb.
- Scale degrees (1 to 7) are the numbers bands use to call notes and chords in any key.
