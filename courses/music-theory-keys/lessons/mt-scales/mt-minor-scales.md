# Minor scales and relative keys

## Why this matters

Plenty of worship songs, Afro ballads and soulful grooves sit in a minor key, and many songs move between major and minor sections. If you can only think in major, a minor song will feel like guesswork. Knowing how minor scales are built, and which major key each one is related to, lets you find the right notes fast and explains why a song in A minor uses the same white keys as C major.

## What makes a scale minor

Like the major scale, a minor scale has seven notes. The difference is where the half steps fall. The most basic version, the **natural minor scale**, follows this formula:

```
Natural minor = W H W W H W W
              = 2 1 2 2 1 2 2   (in half steps, total 12)
```

Compare it with major:

```
Major:          W W H W W W H
Natural minor:  W H W W H W W
```

The minor scale has a **lower 3rd, 6th and 7th** than the major scale on the same root. The lowered 3rd is the note that does most of the work: it is what makes minor sound darker or more serious.

> **Try it:** Play C major (C D E F G A B C), then play C natural minor (C D Eb F G Ab Bb C). Listen to the 3rd: E sounds bright, Eb sounds darker.

## A natural minor

Start on A and apply W H W W H W W:

```
A  -W->  B  -H->  C  -W->  D  -W->  E  -H->  F  -W->  G  -W->  A

A natural minor: A  B  C  D  E  F  G
```

All white keys. A minor uses exactly the same notes as C major, just starting from A.

## Relative major and minor

That is not a coincidence. Every major key has a minor key that uses **the same seven notes**. They are called **relative** keys.

```
C major:   C  D  E  F  G  A  B
A minor:               ...A  B  C  D  E  F  G
                          ^
              start on the 6th note of C major
```

There are two ways to find the relative minor of a major key:

```
1. It is the 6th note of the major scale.
2. It is 3 half steps (a minor third) below the major root.
```

And to go the other way, from minor to major:

```
1. It is the 3rd note of the natural minor scale.
2. It is 3 half steps above the minor root.
```

Worked examples:

```
Relative minor of C:   6th of C major = A.        Check: C down 3 = B, Bb, A.
Relative minor of G:   6th of G major = E.        Check: G down 3 = F#, F, E.
Relative minor of F:   6th of F major = D.        Check: F down 3 = E, Eb, D.
Relative major of D minor: D up 3 = Eb, E, F.     So F major.
```

When you count three half steps, use the letter rule from the last lesson to name the result: the relative minor is always **two letters** below the major root (C to A, G to E, Bb to G). That is how you know the relative minor of Eb major is **C** minor, and the relative minor of F# major is **D#** minor, not Eb minor.

```
Major   C   G   D   A   E   F   Bb  Eb
Minor   A   E   B   F#  C#  D   G   C
```

> **Note:** Relative keys share a key signature, which is why the next lesson can treat them together. A song in E minor and a song in G major are both written with one sharp.

## Relative versus parallel

Do not confuse **relative** with **parallel**:

```
Relative: same notes, different root.   C major <-> A minor
Parallel: same root, different notes.   C major <-> C minor
```

Songs that switch from a major verse to a minor bridge often move to the relative minor, because it shares notes and sounds close. A switch to the parallel minor (C major to C minor) is more dramatic, because three notes change.

## Harmonic and melodic minor

In practice, minor songs often raise the 7th note so it sits a half step below the root, giving a strong pull home. That gives two more versions of the minor scale:

```
A natural minor:   A  B  C  D  E  F   G   A
A harmonic minor:  A  B  C  D  E  F   G#  A    (raised 7th)
A melodic minor:   A  B  C  D  E  F#  G#  A    (raised 6th and 7th, going up)
```

**Harmonic minor** is why, in a song in A minor, you will often hear an E major chord (E G# B) rather than E minor: the G# leads strongly back to A. The gap between F and G# is three half steps, which gives harmonic minor its distinctive, slightly exotic sound.

**Melodic minor** smooths that gap by raising the 6th as well. In classical teaching it is played that way going up and as natural minor coming down.

You do not need to master these yet. Know that the natural minor is the base, and that the raised 7th is a common change.

> **Try it:** Play A natural minor up and down. Then play A harmonic minor (with G#). Then end on A after playing E G# B together. Hear how the G# wants to resolve up to A.

## Hearing major and minor

The quickest test when working out a song is to find the home note, then listen to the 3rd above it:

```
Home note A, 3rd is C#  ->  A major
Home note A, 3rd is C   ->  A minor
```

Hum the home note, then play both options and see which one fits the melody. This is a skill you will develop properly in the listening section.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting three half steps up instead of down.** The relative minor is three half steps **below** the major root. From C, go down to A, not up to Eb.
- **Spelling with the wrong letter.** The relative minor is two letters below the root. Eb major's relative is C minor; F# major's is D# minor.
- **Mixing up relative and parallel.** A minor is the relative of C major. C minor is the parallel.
- **Thinking every raised 7th is a mistake.** A G# in an A minor song is normal; it comes from harmonic minor.
- **Assuming minor means sad.** Minor keys are used for every kind of song, including upbeat dance tracks. It is a colour, not a mood rule.

## Recap

- Natural minor = W H W W H W W.
- Every major key has a relative minor with the same notes: the 6th degree, or three half steps down.
- The relative major of a minor key is three half steps up (its 3rd degree).
- C/A minor, G/E minor, D/B minor, F/D minor and Bb/G minor are pairs to know by heart.
- Harmonic minor raises the 7th; melodic minor raises the 6th and 7th going up.
