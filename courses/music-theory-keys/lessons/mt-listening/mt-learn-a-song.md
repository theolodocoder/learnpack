# Learn a song with theory

## Why this matters

This is where the whole course comes together. A choir director sends a voice note on Friday and says "we're doing this on Sunday". A producer hears a groove and wants to build a beat around the same chords. In both cases you have a recording and no chart. With the method in this lesson you can go from a recording to a written chart in numbers, in any key, with the groove written down too. The video clip "Putting it all together" shows this kind of thinking at the keyboard.

## The method in one picture

```
1. Find the key note      (where does the song rest?)
2. Follow the bass        (what note is under each chord?)
3. Turn bass into numbers (1, 4, 5, 6 ...)
4. Add the qualities      (major or minor?)
5. Map the sections       (verse, chorus, bridge)
6. Find the groove        (4/4, 6/8, 12/8? tempo?)
7. Write the chart        (numbers + key + groove)
```

Do the steps in order. Each one makes the next easier.

## Step 1: find the key note

Play the song and hum along. Find the note that sounds like home: the note where a phrase could end and feel finished. Then find that note on the keyboard by trying notes one at a time while the song plays.

Tips that make this quicker:

- **Listen to the end of the chorus.** Phrases usually land on home there.
- **Hum the last note of the song.** Most songs end on the 1.
- **Try the note against the whole song.** If you hold the right key note, it will fit, or at least not clash, most of the time.

When you have it, that note is your 1. Write down the key: for example, "Key: A".

> **Try it:** Choose a simple song you know well. Play it and hum the last note. Find it on the keyboard. That is very likely the key.

## Step 2: follow the bass

Most songs make the chords clear through the bass line. Listen to the lowest notes and find each one on the keyboard with your left hand. You do not need the whole chord yet, just the bass note at each chord change.

```
Example (song in A), bass notes heard in the verse:
A  ...  E  ...  F#  ...  D  ...
```

If the bass is hard to hear, try headphones, or listen to the left hand of the piano. Many phone apps and DAWs can slow a recording down without changing its pitch, which helps a lot.

## Step 3: turn the bass into numbers

Write out the scale of the key and number it:

```
A major:   A   B   C#  D   E   F#  G#
           1   2   3   4   5   6   7
```

Now translate the bass notes:

```
Bass:     A   E   F#   D
Number:   1   5   6    4
```

That is a 1-5-6-4. You have seen it before. Most songs are built from a small set of common progressions, and once you write them as numbers you start to recognise them everywhere.

## Step 4: add the qualities

Start from the default pattern for a major key: 1, 4, 5 major; 2, 3, 6 minor. Play the default chords along with the recording and listen for any that clash.

```
Default in A:   A   E   F#m   D
```

If one sounds wrong, try the other quality, or try a seventh. Gospel and neo-soul songs often add sevenths and other colours, so play the plain triad first, then experiment.

> **Note:** Sometimes the bass note is not the root of the chord. If the bass walks C#-D under an A chord, the first chord may be "A with C# in the bass", written A/C#. Don't worry about these at first: get the main chords, then refine.

## Step 5: map the sections

Songs repeat. Find the sections and write each one's numbers once.

```
Verse:    1 - 5 - 6 - 4
Chorus:   4 - 1 - 5 - 6
Bridge:   2 - 5 - 1
```

In the key of A that becomes:

```
Verse:    A   E   F#m   D
Chorus:   D   A   E     F#m
Bridge:   Bm  E   A
```

Then write the order of the sections: Intro, Verse, Chorus, Verse, Chorus, Bridge, Chorus, Outro. Note any key change, for example "last chorus up a half step".

## Step 6: find the groove

From the rhythm lessons:

- tap your foot and count: how many beats in a bar?
- does each beat split in two (simple time) or in three (compound time, like 6/8 or 12/8)?
- how fast is it? Tap along with a metronome app's "tap tempo" button to get the beats per minute.

Write it at the top: "4/4, 92 bpm" or "12/8, slow, about 60 beats per minute".

## Step 7: write the chart

A complete number chart fits on one page:

```
Song: (title)              Key: A        Groove: 4/4, 92 bpm

Intro:    | 1 | 5 | 6 | 4 |
Verse:    | 1 | 5 | 6 | 4 |  x2
Chorus:   | 4 | 1 | 5 | 6 |  x2
Bridge:   | 2 | 5 | 1 | 1 |
Outro:    | 4 | 5 | 1 | 1 |
```

Because it is in numbers, the same chart works if the singer needs it in G or Bb. Write the key at the top and change only that.

## Checking your work

Play your chart along with the recording, start to finish. Mark every place that sounds wrong and fix those spots. Then record yourself playing it alone and listen back. If you can hear the song without the original, your chart is right.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with chords before the key.** Find the 1 first; everything else depends on it.
- **Listening to the melody instead of the bass.** The melody moves around; the bass usually tells you the chord.
- **Writing chord names only.** Write numbers too, so you can play it in any key.
- **Ignoring the groove.** A right chord in the wrong feel still sounds wrong. Note the time signature and tempo.
- **Copying lyrics into your chart.** Use section names and bar lines. Lyrics belong to the songwriter, and you don't need them to play the song.

## Recap

- Find the key note first: where the song rests and ends.
- Follow the bass line to find each chord's root.
- Turn the bass notes into numbers using the scale of the key.
- Start with the default qualities (1, 4, 5 major; 2, 3, 6 minor) and adjust by ear.
- Map the sections and their order, then add the groove: time signature and tempo.
- Write a number chart with the key at the top so you can play it in any key.
