# Ear training you can do every day

## Why this matters

Many church keyboardists learnt everything by ear, and that ear is a real skill. This course has given you names for what you hear. Ear training joins the two, so that when the choir starts a song you already half-know the numbers before your hands move. For producers, a trained ear means fewer hours clicking through chord presets to find the one you can hear in your head. A few minutes a day is enough; what matters is doing it often.

## What "a good ear" is made of

A good ear is not one gift. It is several separate skills, and each one can be trained:

```
Skill                        Question you can answer
Chord quality                Is this chord major or minor?
Intervals                    How far apart are these two notes?
Scale degrees                Which number of the key is this note?
Chord function               Is this chord the 1, 4, 5 or 6?
Rhythm                       Is this in 4/4, 3/4, 6/8 or 12/8?
```

Train them one at a time. Trying to hear everything at once is what makes ear training feel impossible.

## Step 1: major or minor

Start with the most useful single skill: telling a major chord from a minor one.

```
C major:  C  E  G      4 + 3 half steps
C minor:  C  Eb G      3 + 4 half steps
```

Only the middle note changes, by one half step. Major tends to sound bright and settled; minor tends to sound darker or more serious. Those words help at first, but do not rely on "happy" and "sad": plenty of joyful worship songs lean on minor chords. The surest way is to compare.

> **Try it:** Play C major, then C minor, then C major again. Do the same on F, then on A. Then close your eyes, play a random root, pick major or minor without looking, and name what you hear before you check.

## Using the major-or-minor video

The video on this lesson is a "major or minor?" ear training game by CSound. It plays chords and gives you a moment to decide before revealing the answer.

Be clear about what it is: **guided listening, not a test.** The video cannot hear your answers or keep score. It is only useful if you commit to an answer before each reveal. A simple routine:

1. Have a pen and paper ready, or a notes app.
2. For each chord, say "major" or "minor" out loud before the answer appears.
3. Mark a tick or a cross.
4. At the end, count your ticks and write the date and score down.

If you get most of them right, move on to intervals. If not, go back to comparing pairs at your own keyboard, where you can hear the same root both ways.

## Step 2: intervals with reference songs

An interval is the distance between two notes. A common trick is to link each interval to the opening of a tune you know well. Choose your own reference songs: a hymn, a children's song, a worship chorus you sing every week. What matters is that the first two notes of the tune make the interval.

```
Interval         Half steps    On the keys from C
Minor 2nd        1             C -> C#
Major 2nd        2             C -> D
Minor 3rd        3             C -> Eb
Major 3rd        4             C -> E
Perfect 4th      5             C -> F
Tritone          6             C -> F#
Perfect 5th      7             C -> G
Major 6th        9             C -> A
Octave           12            C -> C
```

> **Try it:** Play C then F. Sing it. Now think of tunes you know that start with that jump. Write one down next to "perfect 4th". Repeat for the 5th, the major 3rd and the octave.

## Step 3: hear the numbers

This is the skill that pays off most in church and band settings. Play the 1 chord of a key to set the "home" sound in your ear, then play a single note from the scale and name its number.

```
Play:   C major chord (sets the key)
Then:   G         -> that's the 5
Then:   A         -> that's the 6
Then:   F         -> that's the 4
```

Each degree has its own feeling against the key. The 1 is home. The 5 is stable but open. The 7 leans hard up to the 1. The 4 often wants to fall to the 3. Once you know these feelings, they are the same in every key.

Do the same with chords: play the 1 chord, then a 4, 5 or 6 chord, and name it. In the next lesson you will use this to work out a whole song.

## Step 4: rhythm

Rhythm is ear training too. While a song plays:

- tap your foot and count the beats in a bar (four? three? two big ones?);
- decide whether each beat splits into two or three;
- try to name the time signature: 4/4, 3/4, 6/8 or 12/8.

## Daily routines

Pick one routine and do it every day for two weeks before changing it.

**Five minutes (on your phone):**

```
2 min   major or minor (an app, the video, or tonedear.com)
2 min   intervals: 4ths, 5ths and octaves only
1 min   sing the 1-2-3-4-5 of any key out loud
```

**Ten minutes (at the keyboard):**

```
3 min   play 1 chord, then a random 1/4/5/6 chord, name it, check
3 min   play 1 chord, then a random scale note, name the number, check
4 min   pick a song you know, find its key note by humming and trying bass notes
```

**During service or rehearsal (no keyboard):**

```
Silently name the number of each bass note the bass player plays.
Notice when the band goes to the 4 or the 6.
Tap and count the time signature of each song.
```

Short, frequent practice works better than one long session a week. Write the date and what you did; you will be able to see progress over a month.

> **Note:** tonedear.com has free ear training drills for chords, intervals and scale degrees, with scores you can track. It is a good partner to the routines above.

## Common mistakes

- **Guessing without committing.** If you do not say your answer before the reveal, you are not training anything.
- **Only practising the easy skill.** Once major and minor are solid, move on to numbers. That is where the payoff is.
- **Relying on "happy" and "sad".** These labels break quickly. Compare and listen for the middle note.
- **Long sessions once a week.** Your ear improves with frequent short sessions.
- **Not singing.** Singing the notes, even quietly, links what you hear to what you know faster than listening alone.

## Recap

- A good ear is several skills: chord quality, intervals, scale degrees, chord function and rhythm.
- Major and minor differ only in the middle note of the chord; train them by comparing.
- The major-or-minor video is guided listening: say your answer before each reveal and keep your own score.
- Link intervals to tunes you already know.
- Hearing scale degrees and chord numbers is the skill that helps most when playing with others.
- Five to ten minutes every day beats an hour once a week.
