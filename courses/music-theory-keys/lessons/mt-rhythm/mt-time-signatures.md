# Time signatures and counting

## Why this matters

Most mistakes in a band are not wrong notes; they are notes in the wrong place. A keyboardist who comes in a beat early on the chorus, or who plays a straight pattern under a swung groove, is heard by everyone in the room. Producers face the same problem in a DAW: if you set the project to 4/4 when the song breathes in 6/8, the grid fights you all the way. Knowing what a time signature means, and how to count it out loud, fixes most of this.

## Beats and bars

Music has a steady pulse, the **beat**, the thing you tap your foot to. Beats are grouped into **bars** (also called measures). The **time signature** at the start of a piece tells you how those beats are grouped.

```
4/4   top number:    4 beats in each bar
      bottom number: each beat is a quarter note
```

The bottom number names a note value:

```
2  = half note
4  = quarter note
8  = eighth note
16 = sixteenth note
```

## Note values as numbers

In 4/4 one beat is one quarter note. Every other note value is a fraction or a multiple of that:

```
Note          Beats in 4/4    Per bar of 4/4
Whole         4               1
Half          2               2
Quarter       1               4
Eighth        0.5             8
Sixteenth     0.25            16
```

A bar of 4/4 is full when its note values add up to 4. Any mix works:

```
2 + 1 + 1                  = 4    (half, quarter, quarter)
1 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 2          = 4    (quarter, two eighths, half)
2 + 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.25  = 4    (half, quarter, eighth, two sixteenths)
```

This is the same sum a drum machine or piano roll is doing for you when it snaps notes to the grid.

> **Try it:** Set a metronome (any free metronome app or website) to 70 beats per minute. Play the note C on every click and count "1, 2, 3, 4". Then play two notes per click and count "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and". Then four notes per click: "1 e and a, 2 e and a…".

## Counting out loud

Counting syllables keeps you in place:

```
Quarters:     1       2       3       4
Eighths:      1   &   2   &   3   &   4   &
Sixteenths:   1 e & a 2 e & a 3 e & a 4 e & a
```

Say it out loud while you play. It feels silly for a week and then it becomes the quickest way to find exactly where a note lands. When a drummer says "the stab is on the and of 2", you will know where that is.

## Simple and compound time

Time signatures fall into two families.

**Simple time:** each beat divides into **two** equal parts. 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 are simple.

```
4/4:   | 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & |
3/4:   | 1 & 2 & 3 & |
```

**Compound time:** each beat divides into **three** equal parts. 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8 are compound. In compound time the top number counts the small notes, and you divide by three to get the beats you actually feel:

```
6/8  ->  6 eighth notes   ->  6 / 3  = 2 felt beats
9/8  ->  9 eighth notes   ->  9 / 3  = 3 felt beats
12/8 -> 12 eighth notes   -> 12 / 3  = 4 felt beats
```

Each felt beat in compound time is a **dotted quarter note** (a quarter plus an eighth, which is three eighths).

## 3/4 versus 6/8

Both bars hold six eighth notes, so on paper they look like the same amount of music. They feel completely different:

```
3/4:  | 1  &  2  &  3  & |      three beats, each split in two
        >     >     >

6/8:  | 1  2  3  4  5  6 |      two beats, each split in three
        >        >
```

3/4 is the waltz: ONE-two-three, ONE-two-three, with a strong first beat and two lighter ones. Many hymns are in 3/4.

6/8 has a rocking, two-beat feel: ONE-and-a TWO-and-a. You could march to it, with each step on one of the two big beats.

A simple test: tap your foot to the song. If your foot falls three times per bar and each tap splits in two, it is 3/4. If your foot falls twice per bar and each tap splits in three, it is 6/8.

> **Try it:** Play the six eighth notes C C C C C C over and over. First accent the 1st, 3rd and 5th (3/4). Then accent only the 1st and 4th (6/8). Same notes, different groove.

## Reading a bar in a chart

Chord charts often show slashes for beats:

```
| C / / / | F / G / | Am / / / | F / G / |
```

In 4/4 each slash is a beat, so `F / G /` means F for two beats, then G for two beats. In 6/8 a chart usually shows two slashes per bar, one for each felt beat. Always check the time signature at the top before counting the slashes.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading 6/8 as "six beats".** You feel two beats, each made of three eighths. Counting six slow beats makes the groove drag.
- **Confusing 3/4 and 6/8.** Same number of eighths, different accents. Let your foot decide.
- **Bars that don't add up.** If a 4/4 bar in your DAW holds notes adding up to 3.5 or 4.5, something is misplaced.
- **Rushing the rests.** A rest counts just as much as a note. Keep counting through it.
- **Not counting at all.** If you get lost, counting out loud is the fastest way back.

## Recap

- The top number of a time signature is how many; the bottom number is which note value.
- In 4/4, half = 2 beats, quarter = 1, eighth = 0.5, sixteenth = 0.25; a full bar adds up to 4.
- Count with "1 & 2 &" and "1 e & a" to place notes exactly.
- Simple time splits beats in two; compound time splits them in three.
- 6/8 has six eighths but two felt beats; 12/8 has twelve eighths and four felt beats.
- 3/4 and 6/8 hold the same amount of music but group it differently.
