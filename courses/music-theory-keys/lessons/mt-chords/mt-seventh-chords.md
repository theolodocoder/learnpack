# Seventh chords and sus chords

## Why this matters

Triads are the skeleton; seventh chords are where the colour lives. The lush sound of gospel keys, the smooth pads under a lot of Afro and R&B production, and the "that chord wants to go home" pull before a chorus all come from four-note chords. Sus chords do a different job: they hold back the third, so the chord floats instead of committing to major or minor. Once you can build these on any root, chord charts full of "maj7", "m7", "7" and "sus4" stop being intimidating.

## From triad to seventh chord

A seventh chord is a triad with one more third stacked on top. The new note is a seventh above the root.

```
root  ->  third  ->  fifth  ->  seventh
C         E          G          B           (C major 7)
```

On white keys, it's still "one, skip one", just one step further: C (D) E (F) G (A) B.

Which seventh you add, major (11 half steps above the root) or minor (10 half steps), combined with the triad underneath, gives each chord its name.

## The four seventh chords you need first

```
Name               Symbol      Triad        7th above root   Gaps      Example on C
major 7            Cmaj7       major        major 7th (11)   4 3 4     C  E  G  B
minor 7            Cm7         minor        minor 7th (10)   3 4 3     C  Eb G  Bb
dominant 7         C7          major        minor 7th (10)   4 3 3     C  E  G  Bb
half-diminished 7  Cm7b5       diminished   minor 7th (10)   3 3 4     C  Eb Gb Bb
```

Read the gaps as "half steps from each note to the next one up". They are the quickest way to build any of these on any root.

### Major 7

Major triad plus a major seventh, which is one half step below the octave. It sounds warm, dreamy and a little jazzy. It's a favourite for slow worship intros and neo-soul.

```
Cmaj7:   C -(4)- E -(3)- G -(4)- B
Fmaj7:   F -(4)- A -(3)- C -(4)- E
```

> **Try it:** Play C major, then add B on top. Now play just C and B together. That major-seventh rub is what gives the chord its shimmer.

### Minor 7

Minor triad plus a minor seventh. It's smooth and relaxed, softer than a plain minor triad. Many Afro, highlife and R&B grooves sit on minor 7 chords for bars at a time.

```
Dm7:   D -(3)- F -(4)- A -(3)- C
Am7:   A -(3)- C -(4)- E -(3)- G
```

### Dominant 7

Major triad plus a minor seventh. This is the chord that wants to resolve. Inside it, the third and the seventh are a tritone apart (in G7, B up to F is 6 half steps), and that tension pulls toward the chord a fifth below.

```
G7:   G -(4)- B -(3)- D -(3)- F
      B and F want to move to C and E: G7 -> C
```

It's called "dominant" because it naturally appears on the fifth note of the major scale, which is called the dominant. When a chart just says "G7" or "D7", it means a dominant seventh, not a major seventh.

> **Note:** The symbol "7" on its own always means dominant 7. A major 7 is always written "maj7" (or "M7" or "Δ7"). Mixing these up is the most common chart-reading mistake.

### Half-diminished 7

A diminished triad plus a minor seventh, written m7b5 (or ø7). In C major it lives on B: B D F A. You'll meet it less often than the other three, but you need it to complete the set of seventh chords in a key. The full **diminished 7** (3 3 3, for example B D F Ab) is a separate, more dramatic chord that gospel players love as a passing chord.

## Seventh chords in a major key

Stack four notes on each degree of C major, white keys only:

```
Degree   Notes       Gaps     Chord
1        C E G B     4 3 4    Cmaj7
2        D F A C     3 4 3    Dm7
3        E G B D     3 4 3    Em7
4        F A C E     4 3 4    Fmaj7
5        G B D F     4 3 3    G7
6        A C E G     3 4 3    Am7
7        B D F A     3 3 4    Bm7b5
```

The pattern is the same in every major key: maj7, m7, m7, maj7, 7, m7, m7b5. The 2-5-1 (Dm7 G7 Cmaj7) built from this table is the backbone of a huge amount of gospel and jazz, and the progressions section comes back to it.

> **Try it:** Play Dm7, G7, Cmaj7 slowly with your right hand while your left plays the roots D, G, C. Listen to how G7 leans into Cmaj7.

## Sus chords

"Sus" is short for suspended. A sus chord replaces the third of a triad with either the second or the fourth. With no third, the chord is neither major nor minor: it sounds open and unresolved.

```
Name    Symbol   Notes from root            Gaps   Example
sus2    Csus2    root, major 2nd, 5th       2 5    C  D  G
sus4    Csus4    root, perfect 4th, 5th     5 2    C  F  G
```

More examples:

```
Asus4:   A -(5)- D -(2)- E
Dsus2:   D -(2)- E -(5)- A
Gsus4:   G -(5)- C -(2)- D
```

The classic move is **sus4 to major**: hold the fourth, then let it fall a half step to the third.

```
Csus4 -> C:    C F G  ->  C E G       (F falls to E)
```

Gospel players also use the **7sus4**, a dominant 7 with the third replaced by the fourth: G7sus4 is G C D F. It's a very common sound just before the band lands on the home chord.

> **Try it:** Play Asus4 (A D E) then A major (A C# E). Then Asus2 (A B E) to A. Hear how both resolve, but differently.

## Building any of these from any root

The method never changes:

1. Find the root.
2. Follow the gaps for the chord type, counting half steps.
3. Name each note, using every other letter where you can (for sus chords, the second or fourth letter replaces the third).

```
Ebmaj7:   Eb -(4)- G -(3)- Bb -(4)- D
F#m7:     F# -(3)- A -(4)- C# -(3)- E
Bb7:      Bb -(4)- D -(3)- F  -(3)- Ab
```

Watch the Marks Piano clips in this lesson (major 7, minor 7, dominant 7, then sus2 and sus4). After each, build the chord on two roots of your own before moving on.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading "C7" as C major 7.** C7 is dominant: C E G Bb. Cmaj7 is C E G B.
- **Adding the 7th by habit, not by counting.** The 7th is 10 or 11 half steps above the root depending on the chord. Count down from the octave: one half step down is a major 7th, two half steps down is a minor 7th.
- **Keeping the third in a sus chord.** Csus4 is C F G. Playing C E F G is a different (and muddier) chord.
- **Calling the chord on 7 in a major key "minor 7".** In C major, B D F A has a diminished triad underneath: it's Bm7b5.
- **Assuming every chord needs a 7th.** Sevenths are colour. In a busy band, the bass or guitar may already cover some notes; sometimes the plain triad sits better.

## Recap

- A seventh chord is a triad plus a third on top.
- Major 7 = 4 3 4, minor 7 = 3 4 3, dominant 7 = 4 3 3, half-diminished 7 = 3 3 4.
- "7" alone means dominant. Major 7 is written "maj7".
- In a major key: maj7, m7, m7, maj7, 7, m7, m7b5.
- Sus2 replaces the third with the 2nd (C D G); sus4 replaces it with the 4th (C F G).
