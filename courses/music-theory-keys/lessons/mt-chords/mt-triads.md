# Major, minor and diminished triads

## Why this matters

Most of the chords in a worship set, a highlife guitar loop or a pop chorus are triads: three notes, stacked in thirds. If you can build a major, minor and diminished triad on any note, you can play from a chord chart, follow a music director calling chord names, and sketch a progression in your DAW without a chord-finder app. It also explains what you've been doing by ear: the shapes your hands already know have formulas behind them.

## What a triad is

A triad is three notes built by stacking two thirds on top of a starting note, called the **root**.

```
root  ->  third  ->  fifth
```

The note names come from their interval above the root: the middle note is a third above the root, and the top note is a fifth above the root. In C, that's C, E and G. On white keys, the shortcut is "play one, skip one": C (skip D) E (skip F) G.

```
| C | D | E | F | G | A | B |
  X       X       X            C major: C  E  G
```

What decides the **quality** of the triad (major, minor or diminished) is the size of each third.

## The three formulas

Measure each triad in half steps: root to third, then third to fifth.

```
Quality       Bottom gap   Top gap   Root to fifth   Example
major         4            3         7               C  E  G
minor         3            4         7               C  Eb G
diminished    3            3         6               C  Eb Gb
```

In words:

- **Major** = major third (4) + minor third (3) on top. Bright, settled, the "home" sound of most praise songs.
- **Minor** = minor third (3) + major third (4) on top. Darker, softer. A lot of Afro and gospel grooves sit on minor chords.
- **Diminished** = minor third (3) + minor third (3). The outer interval is a tritone (6), not a perfect fifth, so it sounds tense and unstable.

Major and minor triads share the same root and fifth. The only difference is the middle note: lower the third of a major triad by one half step and it becomes minor.

```
C major:   C   E   G      gaps 4, 3
C minor:   C   Eb  G      gaps 3, 4
                ^ one half step lower
```

> **Try it:** Play C major, then move only your middle finger down one key to Eb. Go back and forth. Do the same on F (F A C to F Ab C) and on G (G B D to G Bb D).

There is a fourth type, the **augmented** triad (4 + 4, for example C E G#), which is rare enough that we'll leave it for later. You'll mostly meet it as a passing chord.

## Building a triad on any root

Use the formula and count. Here's a major triad on D, worked through key by key:

```
Start on D.
Up 4 half steps:  D -> D# -> E -> F -> F#     third = F#
Up 3 more:        F# -> G -> G# -> A           fifth = A
D major = D  F#  A
```

Notice that D major needs a black key. If you just played "one, skip one" on white keys (D F A) you'd get D minor, because D to F is only 3 half steps.

More worked examples:

```
A minor:        A  -> (3) -> C  -> (4) -> E      A  C  E
E minor:        E  -> (3) -> G  -> (4) -> B      E  G  B
F major:        F  -> (4) -> A  -> (3) -> C      F  A  C
B diminished:   B  -> (3) -> D  -> (3) -> F      B  D  F
Bb major:       Bb -> (4) -> D  -> (3) -> F      Bb D  F
```

> **Note:** Spell triads with every other letter: a chord on D uses the letters D, F and A, so D major is D F# A, not D Gb A. The pitches sound the same, but the letter spelling makes the third and fifth easy to read. The practice checks accept either spelling.

## Chord symbols

On a chart, triad qualities are written like this:

```
C       C major          (a letter on its own means major)
Cm      C minor          (also written C-, Cmin)
Cdim    C diminished     (also written C°)
Caug    C augmented      (also written C+)
```

So "Am" means A C E, "F#m" means F# A C#, and "Bdim" means B D F.

## Triads from the major scale

Stack "one, skip one" on each note of the C major scale, using only white keys, and you get seven triads. Measure their gaps and a pattern appears:

```
Root   Notes     Gaps   Quality      Symbol
C      C E G     4 3    major        C
D      D F A     3 4    minor        Dm
E      E G B     3 4    minor        Em
F      F A C     4 3    major        F
G      G B D     4 3    major        G
A      A C E     3 4    minor        Am
B      B D F     3 3    diminished   Bdim
```

Major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished. That pattern is the same in every major key, and it is the reason the number system works. The progressions section builds directly on this table, so it is worth playing through now.

> **Try it:** Play all seven triads in C, one after the other, up the keyboard. Then do the same in G major (G Am Bm C D Em F#dim) and check that the qualities follow the same order.

## Hearing the difference

Watch the "Chords" and "Major and minor" clips from Andrew Huang's video (over 10 million views, and a good fast overview), then the "Diminished chords" clip from Marks Piano. After each clip, play the chords yourself before going to the practice.

A good ear exercise: have a friend (or a random-chord app) play a triad, and decide if it's major, minor or diminished before you look. Start with just major versus minor. Diminished stands out once you know it: it sounds like it wants to fall somewhere.

In church, you'll often hear a diminished chord used as a passing chord between two stable chords, for example moving up from one chord to the next by a half step in the bass. Notice it when it happens, and later we'll name the common moves.

## Common mistakes

- **Playing only white keys.** "One, skip one" on white keys only gives the right quality in C major. D major, E major and A major all need a black key for the third.
- **Getting the gaps in the wrong order.** Major is 4 then 3. Minor is 3 then 4. Both add up to 7, so the fifth is the same; only the middle note moves.
- **Lowering the fifth instead of the third for minor.** C minor is C Eb G. C E Gb isn't a standard triad at all.
- **Forgetting B diminished.** B D F looks like an ordinary white-key triad, but B to F is a tritone. In C major, the chord on B is diminished, not minor.
- **Reading "C" as just the note.** On a chord chart, a plain letter means the major triad on that root.

## Recap

- A triad is a root, a third and a fifth, stacked in thirds.
- Major = 4 + 3, minor = 3 + 4, diminished = 3 + 3 half steps.
- Major and minor differ only by the middle note, one half step apart.
- Count the half steps from the root rather than trusting white-key shapes.
- The triads in a major key always go: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished.
- Next lesson: add a fourth note on top and you get seventh chords.
